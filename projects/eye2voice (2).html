UC Berkeley Top Capstone Project

Eye2Voice

Eye2Voice is a real-time, sight-to-speech tool for people who cannot communicate verbally. A phone camera reads the user's gaze direction (up/down, left/right) to make a selection.

The Problem

Each year in the United States, 5.4 million people experience physical paralysis. Among children with autism, 1 in 4 are non-verbal or minimally verbal. For people who cannot speak, the tools that help are out of reach for most. Dedicated communication devices cost around $12,000 and take weeks to set up. They also depend on hardware that can break or wear out.

Our Solution

Eye2Voice addresses that gap. It runs in the browser on a device the user already owns. In conversation mode, a caregiver poses a question by voice or text, and a language model generates a set of possible responses. The user answers by looking Up, Down, Left, or Right to choose one.

The Approach

We trained the model on two public datasets. The primary source was MIT's GazeCapture, about 850,000 samples collected on everyday phones and tablets. That range of lighting, head position, and device angle gave the model what it needed to generalize. We added ETH-XGaze to cover extreme head poses and wider gaze angles the first dataset handled poorly.

The model classifies direction rather than exact gaze coordinates. This fits users who cannot hold a steady gaze, and it removes the need for special hardware or calibration.

GazeNet reads three crops from each frame: the face and each eye. It also takes seven geometric measurements from facial landmarks, such as iris position and head angle, as numbers rather than pixels.

We built GazeNet as a convolutional neural network, following the iTracker model that did this successfully on GazeCapture. The inputs run through three branches. A shared CNN reads both eyes for gaze direction and pupil state. A second CNN reads the face for head orientation. A small network handles the geometric measurements. The three branches merge into one vector, and a final classifier scores each direction. The highest score is the prediction.

The Challenges

Two challenges shaped the work. The first was persistent overfitting, a gap between training and validation accuracy that indicated the model was memorizing rather than generalizing. Uneven participant data was one suspected factor, since some people contributed as few as 18 frames and others several thousand. Balancing those frame counts narrowed the gap only partway. The second challenge was inconsistent class identification. The Up direction was recognized far less reliably than the others, as it made up only about 3% of the training data. Several measures helped, including augmentation, loss-level adjustments, and supplemental Up samples. The most effective single change was removing the Straight (center) class, a persistent source of error and unnecessary for a four-direction interface. That one change improved both problems at once, raising Up recall and narrowing the overfitting gap.

The Evaluation

We built and tested 60 model configurations across three forms of evaluation. On a held-out GazeCapture test set, we measured accuracy, macro F1, and per-class recall and precision. Recall indicates how often a user's intended direction registers, and precision how often the model acts on an unintended one. Promising candidates were then tested live in an application that prompted 24 randomized directions. It recorded both accuracy and time to a confident prediction. Finally, a small usability study placed the tool with domain experts, including a speech-language pathologist and a caregiver.

The Results

Overfitting peaked at roughly 17%, too high for assistive technology. By the final version, the model improved across the board:

  • Test accuracy: 75% → 92%
  • Overfitting (train-validation gap): ~17% → ~4%
  • Recall by direction:
    • Up: 84% (up from ~61%)
    • Down: 93%
    • Left: 92%
    • Right: 91%

Recognition

Eye2Voice received the Hal R. Varian MIDS Capstone Award, given to top projects each semester and named in honor of Hal R. Varian, founding dean of the Berkeley School of Information and former chief economist at Google. Projects are judged on originality, potential impact, completeness, evaluation, presentation, and overall quality. Eye2Voice is a live product under active development, so the code is private.
