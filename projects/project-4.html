Predicting California Wildfires

Using machine learning to predict California wildfire occurrence across 3, 7, and 14-day horizons, combining 40 years of weather and fire data with geospatial feature engineering.

Problem

California wildfires cause billions in damage annually and are intensifying with climate change. Early warning systems that can predict fire occurrence days in advance would support evacuation planning, resource allocation, and policy decisions. Existing ML approaches in the literature have shown strong results for binary fire/no-fire prediction, but most operate at a broad geographic level without accounting for regional climate variation within the state.

We asked whether ML models could predict not just whether a fire would occur, but where in California it would occur, at the climate-division level, across three time horizons. This turned a binary classification problem into an 8-class one (7 climate divisions + no fire), which is a fundamentally harder task with a 12.5% random baseline.

Approach

The dataset combined a Zenodo weather archive (1984-2025) with Cal Fire historical fire perimeters and NASA POWER localized meteorological data. Fire locations were geolocated to California's 7 CONUS climate divisions using Shapely to compute fire centroids per division and GeoPandas to map features to counties and regions. The final merged dataset contained 20,710 rows and 108 features covering precipitation, temperature (min/max/range), wind speed and direction, humidity, and lagged versions of each.

We evaluated multiple model families against a logistic regression baseline: feedforward neural networks with Optuna hyperparameter tuning, Random Forest with PCA and SMOTE variants, XGBoost with randomized search, and ARIMA/SARIMAX for time series. Data was split temporally rather than randomly to prevent leakage from future weather patterns.

Two characteristics of the data made this especially challenging. First, class imbalance: 64% of records were no-fire days. Second, geographic imbalance: fire prevalence varied nearly 9-fold across climate regions, from 178 events in the Northeast Interior Basins to 1,562 in the San Joaquin Drainage.

Results

All models converged around 50% accuracy for the 3-day horizon, well above the 12.5% random baseline but well below what a production early warning system would require. Performance degraded consistently at longer horizons across every model family.

The convergence itself was the most informative result. When logistic regression, neural networks, tree-based ensembles, and time series models all land in the same accuracy range, the bottleneck is likely the data rather than the model. The features available (weather and historical fire occurrence) may not contain enough signal to predict fire location at the climate-division level, particularly for regions with very few fire events in the training set.

Temperature and relative humidity emerged as the strongest predictors across all models. EDA confirmed this visually: fire days clustered at higher temperatures and lower humidity across every climate region. Wind speed showed weaker but consistent separation between fire and no-fire conditions.

The key takeaway was that the 8-class regional prediction problem needs richer features to move beyond ~50% accuracy. Vegetation indices, soil moisture, power infrastructure data, and higher-resolution geographic granularity are likely prerequisites for a production-grade model. The team identified LSTM networks and seasonal statistical approaches as future directions for capturing the temporal dependencies that the current models struggled with.
