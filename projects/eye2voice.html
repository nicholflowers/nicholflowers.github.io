Capstone Deep Learning Computer Vision Assistive Technology

Deep Learning & Computer Vision for Assistive Technology

Eye2Voice is a real-time, sight-to-speech tool for people who have lost the use of their hands and voice. A phone camera reads the user's gaze, and four directions (Up, Down, Left, Right) become the interface.

Problem

For people living with ALS, stroke, or severe motor impairment, losing speech doesn't mean losing the will to communicate, only the means to do it. The assistive devices that restore it are out of reach for most: they can cost $12,000, take weeks to arrive, and require specialized hardware or careful pre-configuration before a single word is spoken. Some don't even run on the devices people already own.

Eye2Voice was built to close that gap. It's a real-time, sight-to-speech tool that turns a device the user already owns into a communication interface, with no specialized hardware and almost no setup. The user looks in one of four directions (Up, Down, Left, Right), and the tool selects a word, phrase, or action. As a team of UC Berkeley data science professionals, we worked across model development, interface design, user research, and evaluation. I built the top-performing models that power the direction classification.

Powering that interface reliably meant solving a hard modeling problem. The training data presented three challenges:

  • Severe class imbalance. The "Up" class was dramatically underrepresented, making the model default to predicting anything but Up.
  • Ambiguous labels. A "Straight" class bled into every other direction, hurting overall accuracy.
  • Participant imbalance. A handful of subjects contributed a disproportionate share of frames, skewing what the model learned.

Approach

A single CNN looking at one full-face photo fails at this problem immediately. In a 112×112 frame, each eye is a tiny patch, and gaze lives in exactly those few dozen pixels of pupil and iris. The information that matters most is the information a whole-image model throws away. So GazeNet, the custom multi-branch network I built (inspired by iTracker), never sees just one image. It takes three: a tight crop of the left eye, a tight crop of the right eye, and the full face.

Each input feeds its own sub-network nested inside the larger model. The two eye crops pass through a single shared eye CNN: the same three convolutional layers process both, on the inductive bias that a left and right eye are structurally the same object, so there's no reason to learn two separate detectors. Weight sharing halves the eye parameters and forces consistent feature extraction across both eyes. The face crop runs through a deeper, separate four-layer CNN that opens with a large 7×7 kernel to downsample fast, then narrows. It isn't reading pupils; it's reading head pose and orientation, the context the eyes alone can't supply. A later version added a third pathway: a small geometric MLP fed facial landmarks directly, handing the model measurements like head angle as numbers rather than asking it to rediscover them from pixels.

The branches stay specialized and only meet at the end. Their outputs (4,608 features from each eye, 2,304 from the face) flatten and concatenate into a single 11,520-dimensional vector, and a fully connected classifier with heavy dropout learns how fine eye detail and broad head pose interact before emitting a confidence score for each of the four directions. This is late fusion by design: keep each pathway doing its own job for as long as possible, and combine only once.

Getting there took more than thirty versions. An early attention-based variant that fused the eyes earlier looked promising on paper and underperformed the simpler shared-weight design. That's the kind of result that only surfaces if you actually build it. But most of the hard-won accuracy came not from the architecture but from the data: removing an ambiguous "Straight" class that bled into every direction, capping each subject at 500 frames so a handful of participants couldn't dominate what the model learned, square-root inverse-frequency class weighting, and supplementing the starved "Up" class with additional frames. Each change was validated against how the model behaved on subjects it had never seen, not just on paper metrics.

The final model reached 92% test accuracy, improving from the mid-70s in early versions, with 81% recall on the hardest class, Up. Most of those gains derived from addressing class imbalance, cleaning labels, and supplementing data rather than from changes to the architecture itself. Crucially, test-set performance did not always predict how the tool felt in use: Left selection was statistically sound yet unreliable in practice, exposing a gap between offline metrics and real-world interaction.
