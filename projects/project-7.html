What Shapes How Long We Sleep?

A multiple regression study on nearly 3,000 CDC survey respondents, testing whether age predicts sleep duration, and a case study in reporting a model honestly when the diagnostics don't cooperate.

Role
Modeling & diagnostics
Team
Three-person group
Stack
R, lm, lmtest, car
Data
CDC NHANES

Question

Sleep is among the strongest predictors of long-term health, yet what actually shapes how long people sleep remains poorly understood. We framed the analysis around a deliberately skeptical null hypothesis: that age has no effect on sleep duration. Failing to reject it would itself have been informative.

We drew data from the CDC's National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), regressing weekday sleep hours on age, gender, an income-to-poverty ratio, and weekly physical activity. After removing incomplete records and engineering the physical-activity variables, the analytic sample comprised 2,991 respondents.

Approach

We began with a parsimonious specification and assessed each predictor before retaining it:

  • Simple model: Sleep ~ Age + Gender + Poverty_Index
  • Complex model: the same, plus log-transformed weekly physical activity

Plotting sleep against age revealed a clearly non-linear relationship: sleep dipped through middle age and rose again toward the extremes. A straight line could not capture that, so I introduced a quadratic Age² term, which proved the turning point of the analysis.

Rather than stop at the coefficients, we subjected each model to a full battery of regression diagnostics:

  • Linearity: residual-vs-fitted plots
  • Homoskedasticity: the Breusch-Pagan test
  • Normality: Q-Q plots and Shapiro-Wilk
  • Multicollinearity: variance inflation factors (VIF)

With the quadratic term included, both Age and Age² were highly significant (p < 1e-14). The data decisively rejected the null hypothesis: age bears a genuine, curvilinear relationship to sleep duration. Gender and physical activity, by contrast, were never significant once the remaining covariates were controlled for.

The essential caveat: although we rejected the null, the model explained only about 2% of the variance in sleep duration. Age is statistically significant but substantively minor; most of what governs how long people sleep lies beyond these predictors.

The diagnostics raised concerns I report rather than omit. Breusch-Pagan indicated pronounced heteroskedasticity (p < 2.2e-16), the Q-Q tails departed from normality, and Age and Age² were severely collinear (VIF ≈ 37). The model is significant, but its standard errors cannot be taken at face value.

What I'd do next

  • Use robust standard errors to handle the heteroskedasticity
  • Center age before squaring it to break the Age / Age² collinearity
  • Add interaction terms and other NHANES variables to capture more of the structure

Takeaway

This project taught me that a near-zero R² and a vanishingly small p-value can coexist, and that the substantive work lies in explaining that tension. A statistically significant result with weak explanatory power and violated assumptions is not a failure to bury; it is the finding, reported with the caveats it warrants. I would rather demonstrate that I understand where a model breaks down than present it as an unqualified success.
